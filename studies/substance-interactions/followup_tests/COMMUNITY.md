# A family or community with water, fog, mist, gas, smoke and clay

Yes: these types can represent people in a family or community. Each person has a place, relationships, needs and a history. The material words give us an expressive way to describe how they respond. A person can combine types or change with the situation.

The meanings below are starting choices for the analogy. They are not claims about any actual family member, and none is inherently the best or worst role.

| Type | Possible meaning in the community | A rule we could test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Water | Adapts while staying connected | Responds readily to nearby members and can reconnect after disruption |
| Fog | Present throughout the group, but difficult to read clearly | Signals reach several members with uncertainty about meaning |
| Mist | Brief, local contact | Makes short interactions that can accumulate over repeated encounters |
| Gas | Needs space and explores outside the immediate group | Maintains fewer local contacts while making occasional distant connections |
| Smoke | Carries traces or messages from earlier events | A past event continues to influence later interactions, even elsewhere |
| Clay | Changes under sustained pressure and retains an impression | Repeated experiences change a slowly recovering memory state |
| Ice | Holds a boundary or stable commitment | Resists small changes, then responds when a chosen threshold is crossed |

For example, one fictional household could have a Water member who reconnects people, a Gas member who wants more space, a Clay member who remembers repeated conflicts, and a Mist member who checks in briefly. Fog and Smoke can describe how information moves between them. These roles can be combined: someone might be Water with friends and Ice when protecting a boundary.

## What connects the people

The useful questions are concrete: Who contacts whom? Who understands the message? Who needs space? Which experiences persist? Who can repair a connection? We can assign each member adjustable contact, openness, boundary and memory variables rather than making the word alone determine their behavior.

One worked scenario to model is a disagreement followed by a quiet period and an invitation to reconnect. Water could reconnect quickly; Gas could return after getting space; Clay could retain the earlier experience; Mist could restore contact through several short visits. Those are chosen rules whose consequences can be compared. The model should also allow the opposite outcomes when the rules or circumstances change.

Success need not mean everyone becoming alike. We could measure whether members maintain desired boundaries, communicate accurately, share a task, recover after disagreement, and preserve distinct preferences.

## How to test this analogy

1. Keep the same people and rules, but exchange the material names. If outcomes change without a rule that reads the names, the implementation has an error.
2. Keep the names but exchange the behavioral rules. This reveals whether an outcome follows memory, contact or boundaries.
3. Move the same member to a different position in the family network. Compare the effect of role with the effect of relationships.
4. Give the same event once and repeatedly; compare immediate response, delayed response and recovery.
5. Repeat across several networks and seeds, preserving individual outcomes as well as group averages.

The existing [SIMS community study](../../social-organization/README.md) already uses Ice, Water, Fog, Mist, Gas and Smoke as symbolic group labels and tests name, memory and boundary effects. Its [recovery and shared-rhythm follow-up](../../sims-recovery-entrainment/README.md) provides more community tests. Adding a family-specific Clay role and the rules above would be a new extension; this guide does not claim that extension has already been simulated.

## How the substance study helps

The physical study offers questions: does a change remain local, spread, persist, reverse or cross a threshold? The community model gives those questions meanings in terms of people and relationships. We can borrow the structure of a memory or transport equation once its variables mean something explicit in the analogy.

For a social rule, justification can simply mean stating the assumption and checking what follows from it. Evidence about actual families would be needed to call it a tested description of human behavior. Water dissolving a gas does not by itself demonstrate reconciliation, and electrical polarization does not by itself demonstrate attraction between people. The analogy remains usable without either claim.
